# OFI prediction: the honest evaluation

AAPL, 2012-06-21, Nasdaq, from LOBSTER's level-10 sample files. Week 6
rows A to D of the plan, and the question the project was built to
answer: does the order flow imbalance over the last interval say
anything about the mid change over the next one, and if it does, does
what remains survive half the spread?

**OFI predicts the next interval's mid change at a horizon of 5 seconds
with a hit rate of 54.3% (plus or minus 2.3 points), and the edge is
consumed by half the spread 11 times over: 0.59 cents gross per signal
against a half-spread of 6.5 cents.** At one second the direction is
right 55.7% of the time on the second half of the day and 49.5% on the
first, so the one-second result does not survive the reversed split and
the five-second one does. Beyond ten seconds nothing clears a coin's
band. The out-of-sample R-squared against the zero forecast is under
0.7% at every horizon. That is roughly what the literature says, and
the rest of this page is the measurement behind each clause.

## What was asked, and how

The question was frozen in the Day 6 guide before any of this ran, and
`src/lob/predict.py` is that specification as code. A horizon h is a
calendar bucket width of 1, 5, 10, 30, 60 or 300 seconds; the predictor
is the OFI over bucket k (Day 5's series), the target is the mid change
over bucket k + 1, and the two windows share an edge and nothing else.
There is one day, so the split is within it: fit on pairs whose target
ends at or before 12:45, test on pairs forecast at or after it, and
then the reverse, so that the open is out of sample in one direction and
the close in the other. The model is through the origin, so no
first-half drift rides along as a forecast (the day fell 803 cents). The
baselines are zero, which every out-of-sample R-squared is measured
against, and the last mid change, fitted the same way, which is the
cheapest thing a trader could do instead. The metrics are the direction
hit rate over test pairs where predictor and target are both nonzero,
with the binomial 95% band around one half for that many calls; the
R-squared against the zero forecast; and the gross edge, the mean
realized move in the forecast direction per signal, in cents, before
any cost.

Regimes are sets of forecast times known at the forecast: the open,
midday and close windows, and the top and bottom deciles of trailing
one-minute realized variance with the cut taken from the first half.
Each is fitted on its complement and tested inside, which is a time
split for the windows and an interleaved one for the deciles. The cost
verdict trades every test-half signal in the forecast direction,
entering at the end of the signal's bucket or one bucket later, and
puts the realized move beside the time-weighted half-spread of the
bucket the trade is entered in (Week 4's `time_weighted`), for every
signal and for the strongest decile of |OFI| with the cut from the fit
half.

Every fit on this page was a row in `results/trials.csv` before it was
a number anywhere, with status `predictive`, its horizon, and its split
named: twenty-four rows for the decay curve, ten for the regimes,
twenty-four for the cost verdict, numbered beside the numbers below.
The registry stood at 19 rows after Day 5 and stands at 77 now, and
Week 10's deflated evaluation divides by that count. The block between
the markers is generated by `scripts/ofi_prediction.py --part writeup`
from the three CSVs and runs no regression.

## What the numbers say

The direction is forecastable for a few seconds and the magnitude is
not. On the primary split the OFI direction is right 55.7% of the time
at one second (z 6.9 over 3,695 calls) and 54.3% at five seconds (z
3.7), both outside the band a coin would produce; at ten seconds and
beyond every hit rate sits inside it. The out-of-sample R-squared
against the zero forecast peaks at 0.68% and is negative at three of
the six horizons, so whatever the sign knows, the size does not. The
predictive slope at one second is 0.51 cents per 1,000 shares, an
eighth of the contemporaneous 4.1 from Day 5: the same thousand shares
of imbalance that moved the mid 4 cents inside their own second are
followed by half a cent in the next one, and by nothing measurable
after ten seconds. The expectations list said a tenth; an eighth is the
first failed line, and the direction of the miss (more carry-over than
expected, not less) is worth remembering when the second name arrives.

The reversed split is the most important table on the page, because it
is the closest thing one day offers to a second day. At five seconds it
agrees (53.9% against 54.3%). At one second it does not: fitted on the
afternoon and tested on the morning, the direction is right 49.5% of
the time, a coin, while the slope is larger and better determined
(0.56 cents per 1,000 shares, t 8.9) and the R-squared is still
positive. The morning's one-second flow carries magnitude information
and no sign information, and the afternoon's carries both. One day
cannot say which half is typical. The sentence names five seconds for
that reason, and the one-second number is reported as what it is: true
of one half of one day.

The baseline is unstable in a way the expectations did not anticipate.
The last mid change was expected to reverse at short horizons; on the
first half it does, barely (gamma -0.009 at one second, t -0.9), and on
the second half it continues (gamma +0.055, t 6.0), so a rule fitted on
either half is wrong on the other, 46% right at one second in both
directions. OFI beats the cheapest alternative by nine points at one
second and by one point at five; the expectation said two points at
most, and that is the second failed line, failed in OFI's favour.

The regimes say where it fails, which at ten seconds is nearly
everywhere. Fitted outside each window and tested inside, the direction
is 56.0% right at the open (180 calls, inside a band of 7.4 points),
52.5% at midday (1,080 calls, inside 3.1), and 47.6% at the close (179
calls, inside 7.5); the ordering written down beforehand (open worst,
close best) came out the other way, which is the fourth failed line.
The one regime that clears its band is the quiet decile: when the
trailing minute's realized variance is in the bottom tenth of the first
half's distribution, the ten-second direction is right 63.6% of the
time over 264 calls (z 4.2), and the last-change baseline is wrong there
(41.4%, below its band). In the high-vol decile the slope is three
times larger and the R-squared is negative. Read with the caveat that
the deciles are interleaved with their fit sets and that 264 calls is
one afternoon's worth, this is the one place the signal looks like a
signal: a quiet book moves the way its flow leans, a busy one does not.

The cost verdict is unambiguous where the counts are large and noise
where they are not. At one second, 6,838 signals in the test half earn
0.36 cents each gross against a half-spread of 6.4 cents at the time,
consumed 18 times over; the net after one aggressive leg is -6.0 cents
per trade and after two it is -12.4. At five seconds, 2,162 signals
earn 0.59 cents gross, consumed 11 times. The strongest decile of
one-second signals (866 trades, an imbalance of at least 531 shares
inside one second) is right 66.2% of the time and earns 1.02 cents, the best edge
on the page, and is still 5.6 times short of the half-spread; one
bucket of latency cuts it to 58.3% and 0.37 cents, and cuts the
five-second edge from 0.59 to 0.06. The rows with fewer than a hundred
signals, which the table marks, include a five-minute "gross edge" of
7.1 cents over 39 trades with a hit-rate band of 16 points and a
reversed-split edge of -4.9 cents; they are the reason the last
expectation line failed as written, and they are noise. The share of
individual trades whose own move beat their own half-spread is 6% at
one second and 16% at five: most trades lose the spread outright, and
the few that beat it do not pay for the rest.

Four of the nine graded lines held and five failed, and the failures
are instructive rather than fatal: the carry-over is an eighth rather
than a tenth, the baseline does not reverse, the one-second result does
not survive the reversed split, the windows are ordered the other way,
and the cost line tripped on horizons with too few trades to mean
anything. The sentence's shape, the line that mattered, held.

## What this does and does not show

It shows that top-of-book OFI on this day carried a small, real,
short-lived directional edge: a few points above a coin for a few
seconds, nothing after ten, and a magnitude too small to see in an
R-squared. It shows that the edge is an order of magnitude smaller than
the cost of taking it. Every assumption in the cost verdict favours the
signal: entry at the exact end of the signal's bucket, an exit at the
mid with no second spread paid, no fees, no queue position, no adverse
selection on the passive leg, no market impact from the trade itself.
Under those assumptions the net is -6 cents per trade. Under realistic
ones it is worse.

It does not show that OFI is useless. The quiet-decile result and the
one-second strongest-decile result are exactly the places a
practitioner would look next, with more days and a passive execution
model in which the signal decides when to post rather than when to
cross. It does not show what happens on a large-tick name, where the
mid moves only when a queue is exhausted and the contemporaneous
relation was stronger in the paper; MSFT is the test of that, when its
files land. And it does not show anything about a second day: the
within-day split is the weakest honest design available, the reversed
direction is its only check, and the one-second disagreement between
the two halves is the measure of how much a second day could change.

One accurately bounded result is the deliverable, and this is it: on
one day of one small-tick name, OFI predicts direction for about five
seconds at 54% and the spread eats the edge eleven times over.

## Limitations

- **One name, one day, one split.** AAPL on 2012-06-21, fit on one half
  and tested on the other. Every number carries that window; the
  reversed split is the only robustness check available, and it
  disagrees at one second.
- **The split is within the day.** A forecast model fitted on the
  morning and tested on the afternoon of the same session shares that
  session's regime; a second day would be a stronger test and is not
  on disk.
- **Small-count rows are noise and are marked.** The five-minute
  horizon has 39 test signals and the strongest-decile rows at 30
  seconds and beyond have 29 or fewer; their edges and hit rates are
  reported because they were logged, not because they mean anything.
- **The cost verdict is optimistic by construction.** Zero latency at
  latency 0, a passive exit at the mid, no fees, no queue, no impact.
  The latency-1 rows are the only concession, and they halve the edge.
- **Classical t-statistics.** They assume independent, homoskedastic
  errors that ten-second mid changes do not have; they are reported as
  the overstatement they are and nothing on this page rests on them.
- **The regimes' deciles are interleaved with their fit sets.** The
  quiet-decile result is the most interesting number here and the one
  with the weakest separation between fit and test.
