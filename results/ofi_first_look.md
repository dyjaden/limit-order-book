# OFI first look: contemporaneous and descriptive

AAPL, 2012-06-21, Nasdaq, from LOBSTER's level-10 sample files; the
order flow imbalance of Cont, Kukanov and Stoikov (2014) computed from
the touch after every message, and the mid-price change over the same
interval regressed on it. Week 5 rows A to D of the plan.

This is the relation inside an interval, between the flow and the price
move that happened together. It is the check that the machinery is
right, not evidence that OFI at time t knows anything about the price at
t + h. A trade that eats the best ask and the uptick it causes are the
same event seen twice, so a strong fit here is expected and proves
nothing about foresight. The predictive question is Week 6's, and its
definition (the horizon, the split, the baseline, the cost) goes into
the registry before it runs.

## What was computed, and under which rules

The flow is `e_n` between consecutive two-sided observations of the
best quotes, in shares, with the sign convention pinned by sixteen
hand-computed tests (`src/lob/ofi.py`, `tests/test_ofi.py`); the
level-1 and level-10 files give the same 107,164 nonzero events at the
same times, so the series does not depend on the filter. The events are
aggregated on two clocks: calendar bins from 09:30, with an empty bin
kept as an observation of zero flow and zero price change, and event
buckets of a fixed number of best-quote updates, which tile the day
from the first observation. A bucket's price change is its closing mid
minus its opening mid, in cents, never a sum of per-event changes.

Each regression is the bucket mid change in cents on the bucket OFI in
shares, fitted two ways: through the origin, which is the paper's
specification (beta is the sum of xy over the sum of x squared), and
with an intercept. Both R-squareds use the centered total sum of
squares, so they are comparable and the origin fit's can never exceed
the intercept fit's. The hit rate is the share of buckets where the
flow and the price change have the same sign, over the buckets where
both are nonzero; the excluded count is the rest, and in the one-second
series that is mostly bins in which nothing at the touch moved. Depth
per half hour is the time-weighted touch depth per side from Week 4's
`time_weighted`, the instrument already validated for that, and the
depth scaling is the regression of log beta on log depth across the
thirteen windows.

The rule that governs this page: every regression below was a row in
`results/trials.csv` before its number was formatted or printed
(`src/lob/registry.py`, pinned by `tests/test_ofi_first_look.py`). The
rows are numbered beside the numbers. A re-run appends nineteen more;
the registry is never rewritten, and Week 10's deflated evaluation
divides by its length.

## What the numbers say

The slope is the stable part. Across the five clocks beta sits between
3.8 and 4.4 cents per 1,000 shares of imbalance, and the intercept is
at most 0.27 cents (at 200 updates) against price changes measured in
tens of cents, so the line goes through the origin as the paper says it
should. The fit tightens as the interval grows: an R-squared of 0.31 at
one second, 0.41 at ten, 0.55 at one minute, which is aggregation
averaging out the noise of a single update. The paper's 0.65 average at
ten seconds was measured mostly on large-tick names, where the mid moves
only when a queue is exhausted; on AAPL's small-tick book the mid also
moves when someone quotes inside the 15-cent spread, which the formula
counts (a new better bid is a whole new queue of buy pressure) but which
makes the touch noisier. The expectation written before the run put
AAPL's R-squared between 0.2 and 0.7 for that reason, and 0.41 landed
inside it. The clock matters little here: 50-update buckets (median
duration 8.4 seconds) give 0.38 against 0.41 for ten-second bins, and
their durations span four orders of magnitude.

The depth scaling has the paper's sign and roughly its size, and little
else can be said of it from one day. Across the thirteen half hours the
slope of log beta on log depth is -1.43, inside the -1.5 to -0.5
written down beforehand, but with an R-squared of 0.27, because the
time-weighted depth per side only ranges from 120 to 192 shares across
the day, a factor of 1.6, which is a short lever for a log-log fit. The
paper estimated its exponent across fifty stocks whose depths differ by
orders of magnitude; the within-day version here is the same question
asked with far less variation to work with, and the second name (MSFT,
pending LOBSTER's request flow) would add the leverage this day lacks.

One sanity check against the paper's stylized model, offered as an
order of magnitude and nothing more. If each side of the touch holds
about D shares and the next level sits one increment behind, the mid
moves half an increment when a queue of D is exhausted, so beta is
about 1 / (2D) increments per share. With D of 150 shares per side that
is 3.3 increments per 1,000 shares; on a dense book an increment is one
cent, which gives 3.3 cents per 1,000 shares against the 4.1 measured.
AAPL's occupied levels average 2.5 cents apart (Day 4), which pushes
the stylized number up, and queues that refill within the interval push
the measured number down; the two effects are not separated here.

The one-second row deserves a reading of its own. Its hit rate is the
highest of the five (90%) and its R-squared the lowest (0.31): at that
scale most bins with any update contain one or two, so the sign is
almost mechanical and the magnitude is almost noise. The 12,300
excluded bins are not missing data; they are seconds in which neither
the flow nor the mid moved, and they stay in the regression as
observations of zero on zero.

## What this does not show

Nothing on this page is a forecast. The regressions pair the flow over
an interval with the price change over that same interval, and the
largest term in that price change is the one the flow itself produced
when it consumed or extended a queue. The hit rate of 86% is the share
of ten-second bins in which the mid moved the way the flow pushed it,
inside the bin, and is not the hit rate of any strategy. Week 6 asks
whether OFI over the last interval says anything about the mid change
over the next one, at 1, 5, 10, 30 seconds, 1 and 5 minutes, against a
baseline, with a time split that keeps the fit and the test apart, and
then whether whatever remains survives half of the spread Day 4
measured at 15 cents. That question is written into the registry
before it is run, and if the answer disagrees with the literature the
answer is the finding.

## Limitations

- **One name, one day.** AAPL on 2012-06-21, the LOBSTER sample. Every
  number here carries that window, and the depth scaling in particular
  wants more than one day and more than one name.
- **The events come from a level-10 file.** The touch is exact in it,
  which is all OFI needs, and the level-1 file gives the same series;
  but the day's first observation is the book after the first message
  at 09:30:00.004, so the first ten-second bin's open is that
  observation, not the opening cross.
- **The contemporaneous fit contains the mechanical part of impact.**
  That is the point of the page, and it is why none of these numbers
  may be quoted as predictability.
- **Depth varies little within the day.** The elasticity of -1.43 is
  estimated across a 1.6-fold range of depth; its R-squared of 0.27
  says how much to trust it.
- **The per-event series is regenerated, not shipped.** `scripts/ofi_report.py`
  writes it from the LOBSTER files in seconds; at one row per message it
  is a transformed copy of licensed data and stays out of the
  repository. The bucket series and the registry are committed.
